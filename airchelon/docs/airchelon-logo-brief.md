# Airchelon — Logo Design Brief

## 1. Brand context (for the designer)

**Name:** Airchelon = Air + Echelon. An echelon is a real aviation formation — aircraft stepped diagonally, close enough to move as one, far enough apart that every pilot keeps a clear, unobstructed line of sight. "Air" adds elevation and perspective.

**Positioning:** A media and technology company built the way a flight formation flies — independent writers and independent products, moving together, each keeping their own line of sight. Subject matter is open (AI, technology, business, creator economy, regulated industries) — the brand is not about any one topic, so the mark shouldn't lean on any one industry's visual clichés (no healthcare crosses, no finance charts, no generic "AI" circuitry).

**Core line:** "Clarity at altitude."

**Personality:** Precise. Elevated. Calm. Quietly confident. Technically fluent without performing it. Collaborative in structure, independent in voice.

---

## 2. Design direction: "Flight deck meets editorial"

Two references, deliberately in tension, both present at once:

- **Flight deck** — the precision of technical instrumentation: schematic line work, instrument-panel gauges, formation diagrams, sectional-chart cartography, cockpit HUD tick marks. Cold, exact, high-trust.
- **Editorial** — the craft of a genuinely good magazine masthead: real typographic character, generous whitespace, a sense of pacing and confidence that comes from print history, not software UI.

The logo should feel like an instrument designed by people who also care about typography — not a SaaS dashboard icon, and not a generic aviation badge. The tension to hold: **instruments are cold and exact; good editorial is warm and considered. The mark should read as both in the same glance.**

---

## 3. Symbol concepts to explore

The etymology gives a direct, ownable visual asset — lean into it rather than defaulting to a generic wordmark-only logo:

1. **The echelon step.** A small number of stepped, diagonal marks (2–4), each offset from the last, each fully visible — not overlapping, not stacked. This is literally what an echelon formation looks like, and it's the most direct, ownable expression of the name. Avoid the cliché "V of geese/jets" silhouette — render it as clean geometric strokes or ticks, closer to a technical diagram than a wildlife logo.
2. **The line of sight.** A single clean diagonal or horizon line, possibly combined with a small mark that suggests a vantage point (a dot, a small chevron) positioned above it — evokes altitude and unobstructed view without drawing a literal horizon/sunset cliché.
3. **Instrument-panel monogram.** An "A" built from the geometry of a real instrument (e.g. an attitude indicator's horizon bar, an altimeter's tick marks) — should read first as a confident letterform, second as an instrument, so it still works small.
4. **Negative-space formation.** Two or three simple shapes (bars, chevrons, or wedges) arranged so the *gaps between them* read as the "line of sight" — sophisticated, but must be tested at small sizes (favicon) to confirm it doesn't collapse into noise.

Pick one direction and take it far, rather than blending all four. The mark needs to work as a tiny, flat-color app icon just as well as it works on a masthead.

---

## 4. Typography direction

Pair two typefaces that embody the same tension as the visual direction:

- **Display/editorial serif** for the wordmark and headlines — something with real character and a print pedigree (think the gravitas of a serious magazine masthead), not a generic system serif. Should feel considered, slightly idiosyncratic, built to be read at large sizes.
- **Precision grotesk or geometric sans** (or a monospace with instrument-panel character) for supporting UI, data, bylines, and captions — should feel like it belongs on a gauge or a technical label, not a friendly startup app.

Avoid rounded, "friendly SaaS" sans-serifs (the Circular/Inter-everywhere look) as the primary wordmark face — that undercuts the editorial half of the direction entirely.

---

## 5. Symbol trademark conflict check (informal)

Before committing to a final mark, an informal image/design-mark search turned up a few real considerations — not blockers, but they should shape the design:

- **Chevron Corporation** holds one of the world's most recognized marks: two stacked blue-and-red V-shaped bars, registered under design-search code **26.3.23** ("lines or bands forming an angle... chevrons and arrowheads") in the US and Canada. A chevron/angle shape by itself is a common, widely-used geometric device (this design code covers many unrelated marks), so the shape isn't off-limits — the risk is specifically **a bold single V rendered in red-and-blue**, which would read as an unmistakable nod to Chevron's mark. Multiple thin, offset diagonal marks (the actual echelon-formation shape) reads nothing like it.
- **Strava** has an active Canadian design-mark filing for an "Echelon Logo" (No. 2350170) tied to their cycling power-zone feature — built from lettering forming an angular figure. Different industry, but worth a direct visual side-by-side once Airchelon's artwork exists.
- **Echelon Fitness Multimedia LLC** holds a registered design mark for a stylized lowercase **"e" with a dot in the opening** — relevant only if pursuing a monogram direction; keep any Airchelon "A" monogram visually distinct from this specific "dotted letter" treatment.
- **Echelon Payments** and **Echelon Risk + Cyber** (two unrelated existing "Echelon" brands) both lean heavily on **blue** as a primary color. Combined with Chevron's blue, this makes blue the de facto color of "Echelon"-adjacent brands — a good practical reason to avoid it as Airchelon's primary hue.
- The "stacked/stepped horizontal layers" motif (levels/rank) appears repeatedly as an unregistered but overused stock-logo cliche for the word "Echelon" — not a legal issue, but a reason to keep favoring the diagonal, line-of-sight aviation reading over the generic "levels" reading.

This is a practical search, not a formal legal clearance — run a proper USPTO/UK IPO design-mark search once real artwork exists, before any commercial launch.

## 6. Color palette

Designed to hold the "flight deck meets editorial" tension and to deliberately sidestep every conflict above: no red+blue (Chevron), no blue-as-primary (Echelon Payments, Echelon Risk + Cyber, and most fitness/sports-tech brands generally). The accent is **amber** — the color of instrument-panel glow and runway edge lighting — which is both on-direction for the aviation half of the brand and structurally distinct from every conflict found.

### Light mode ("paper")

| Role | Hex | Usage |
|---|---|---|
| Background / Paper | `#F7F4EE` | Primary background — warm, print-like white, not stark white |
| Surface | `#FBF9F5` | Cards, containers |
| Border / Hairline | `#DDD8CC` | Dividers, rules |
| Text / Ink | `#1B1A1E` | Primary text |
| Text muted / Slate | `#6E6B62` | Secondary text, captions |
| Accent / Instrument Amber | `#B8631A` | Logo mark, links, CTAs — use sparingly, large elements only |
| Accent hover | `#965013` | Hover/active state |

### Dark mode ("flight deck at night")

| Role | Hex | Usage |
|---|---|---|
| Background / Ink | `#141316` | Primary background |
| Surface | `#1C1B1F` | Cards, containers |
| Border / Hairline | `#34323A` | Dividers, rules |
| Text / Bone | `#EDEAE2` | Primary text |
| Text muted | `#9C9890` | Secondary text |
| Accent / Instrument Amber (bright) | `#E08A34` | Logo mark, links, CTAs — reads like a lit gauge against the dark panel |
| Accent hover | `#F2A253` | Hover/active state |

**Usage rules:**
- Amber is the *only* accent — no second competing hue. It should appear on the logo mark itself and sparingly elsewhere (a single underline, a CTA, a highlighted data point), never as a full-bleed background.
- The amber values are tuned separately for light and dark backgrounds so the accent keeps sufficient contrast in both (deeper `#B8631A` on light paper, brighter `#E08A34` on dark ink) — do not reuse one hex across both modes.
- Reserve amber for large elements (logo, headlines, icons) rather than small body text, where its contrast against light paper is closer to the 3:1 large-text threshold than the 4.5:1 body-text threshold.
- **Avoid:** blue as a primary or dominant color, red+blue together, gradients, glossy Web 2.0 treatments, and multi-color "friendly startup" palettes.

---

## 7. What to avoid

- Literal airplane silhouettes, wings, or clip-art jets — reads as a dated aviation-startup cliché, and undersells the editorial half of the brand.
- The generic "V-formation of birds/jets" logo cliché overused in logistics and consulting branding — if using the echelon motif, render it as a technical diagram, not a wildlife silhouette.
- Anything gunmetal, tactical, or "defense-tech" in color or shape — **Airchelon must look and feel clearly distinct from Aechelon Technology**, a real defense/flight-simulation company with a near-identical name spoken aloud. No shared visual register (no camo tones, no military stencil type, no radar-sweep motifs).
- Abstract swirl/orbit "innovation" logos with no connection to the name.
- Anything that only works as a large hero mark and falls apart at favicon size — every direction must be tested small before it's presented.

---

## 8. Reference moodboard categories (for the designer to pull from, not to copy)

- Aviation sectional charts and navigation charts — clean cartographic line work, restrained color-coding.
- Cockpit instrument panels — attitude indicators, altimeters, gauge typography.
- Classic magazine mastheads with real typographic authority (the kind of gravitas found in long-running serious print publications, not tech blogs).
- Technical blueprint/schematic drawings — precise, unornamented line work.
- Formation-flying diagrams from flight manuals — the literal echelon shape, treated as a diagram rather than an illustration.

---

## 9. Deliverables & specs

- **Primary lockup** — symbol + wordmark, horizontal, for the masthead/homepage header.
- **Stacked lockup** — symbol above wordmark, for square/social contexts.
- **Standalone symbol** — must work as a flat, single-color mark at favicon size (16–32px) and as a social avatar crop.
- **Wordmark-only version** — for contexts where the symbol isn't needed (legal/letterhead, plain-text-adjacent placements).
- **Monochrome versions** — full black and full white, for print, dark mode, and low-contrast placements.
- **Minimum size and clear-space guidelines** documented alongside the final files.
- **File formats:** vector master (SVG/AI/EPS), transparent PNG at multiple sizes, favicon (ICO/SVG), social avatar crop (square, circle-safe).

---

## 10. Success criteria

A strong result will:
- Be immediately traceable to "echelon" once explained, without needing an airplane drawn on it.
- Read as confident and editorial, not as a tech-dashboard icon.
- Hold up in flat, single-color, and small-size use — not just as a large hero graphic.
- Look nothing like Aechelon Technology's visual register.
- Feel like it could sit on a magazine masthead and an instrument panel at the same time — the actual test of "flight deck meets editorial."
